      Purpose

      Following self-determination theory, the purpose of this paper is to investigate the impact of four motivational profiles (external, introjected, identified regulation, and intrinsic motivation) on work performance (interpersonal, adaptive, task, and dedicative performance). The authors also examined the proposed relations with longitudinal data.

      TOKEN

      Participants in Studies 1 and 2 were from several companies in China. Employees completed the questionnaires to measure their work motivation, and managers completed the questionnaires to assess the subordinates’ work performance.

      Findings

      In Study 1, the authors found that identified regulation significantly predicted interpersonal performance and adaptive performance. External regulation, introjected regulation, and intrinsic motivation had no significant impacts on interpersonal, adaptive, task, or dedicative performance. In Study 2, the results revealed that identified regulation significantly predicted dedicative and interpersonal performance, but external regulation, introjected regulation, and intrinsic motivation had no significant impacts on the four types of performance. These two studies concluded that only identified regulation strongly predicts work performance.

      Originality/value

      The study has contributed to the body of knowledge by clarifying that identified regulation is an important type of motivation in the workplace. Managers might therefore focus on supporting employees for identifying with the organizational goals in order to promote better performance.

      Introduction

      Extant literature on work performance has suggested that employees’ motivation is one of the key factors in promoting work performance (Pinder, 2011). As such, managers seek effective ways to support the motivation of their employees in order to improve employees’ work performance (Imran et al., 2014). According, the relationship between motivation and work performance has received considerable empirical attention in industrial and organizational psychology for many years (Steers et al., 2004). Although the exact understanding of motivation continues to evolve (Kanfer et al., 2012), most of the theories have described motivation as either extrinsic or intrinsic and then explored its effects on work performance (Cerasoli et al., 2014). According to those theories, extrinsically motivated behaviours are governed by an external mechanism (e.g. incentives or punishment), whereas intrinsically motivated behaviours are directed by personal interest (Cerasoli et al., 2014). However, extant literature has suggested that sometimes individuals’ behaviour cannot be well explained by either intrinsic interest or extrinsic incentives (Vansteenkiste et al., 2010). For instance, employees may engage in work activities because they feel responsibility for their work or because they identifies with the importance of the work rather than because they feel interested in their work (i.e. intrinsically motivated) of pressured to do it (external regulation). This sense of identifying the work as important is also a type of motivation within self-determination theory (SDT) and could therefore also promote various work outcomes (Burton et al., 2006; Deci and Ryan, 2000).

      SDT was built on the classic distinction between extrinsic and intrinsic motivation and presents a continuum model of controlled vs autonomous (Deci and Ryan, 2000). According to the continuum model, extrinsic motivation can be differentiated into multiple regulation types: extrinsic regulation, introjected regulation, and identified regulation (Gagné et al., 2015). This multidimensional conceptualization of motivation and thus provides a more nuanced way to explore the relationship between motivation and work performance. SDT suggested that external regulation is the least self-determined type of extrinsic motivation, with introjected and identified being progressively more autonomous or self-determined. The degree to which an extrinsic motivation has been internalized determines the degree to which it is autonomous and thus influences the work outcomes that result (Gagné and Deci, 2005; Vansteenkiste et al., 2006; Deci et al., in press). Compared with extrinsic motivation, intrinsic motivation is inherently autonomous and has more positive effects on well-being and performance (Deci and Ryan, 2000). SDT studies have indicated that more self-determined motivations (e.g. intrinsic motivation and identified regulation) are associated with greater persistence, academic performance, social functioning, and physical and psychological wellness in the field of academics and sports (Black and Deci, 2000; Sheldon and Kasser, 1998; Williams et al., 1996). But it is also the case that intrinsic and identified regulations have somewhat different relations to academic performance (Burton et al., 2006). We argue that the theory and empirical results can aid in an exploration of the relationships among the motivation types and work performance in the work domain.

      Some have argued that, in the workplace, job performance is more closely related to extrinsic motivation than intrinsic motivation (Lazear, 2000) because most people work to earn a living. Thus, using monetary rewards and punishments as a central motivational strategies seem practical and appealing, and they are widely integrated into management systems in today’s enterprises (Benabou and Jean, 2003). This indicates that managers are likely to consider extrinsic motivation as a core factor in promoting followers’ performance. Second, most managers believe that contingent rewards serve as “positive reinforcements” for desired behaviours (Grant and Shin, 2011) because jobs are likely boring and lacking in interests value, so managers would view supporting intrinsic motivation as impractical. Further, employees’ personal interest may not be directly related to what they need to do to perform well at work, and their goals may not be align with those of the organization. Thus, when an individual’s goals do not fit the organization’s goal, intrinsic motivation may at times fail as a motivational strategy for promoting work performance, although, empirical evidence does indicate that more autonomous motivation does on average promote more effective performance, especially high-quality performance (Cerasoli et al., 2014).

      The purposes of this paper are to examine the relations from intrinsic motivation and types of extrinsic motivation to performance in the work domain. Our first contribution is to advance previous research by exploring the relations among detailed motivation types and work performance in the work domain. Second, this research aim to examine if well-internalized extrinsic motivation (i.e. identified regulation) is a stronger predictor of work performance than intrinsic motivation. And we also attempt to solve the conflict between managers’ common knowledge, i.e. extrinsic regulation is more important in predicting performance, and the SDT’s view, i.e. intrinsic motivation has a more positive effect on work outcomes (Deci and Ryan, 2000). Finally, we also contribute to investigate and report each motivational profile over time to identify the effect of employee motivation changes on work performance. In the following section we describe the theory and hypotheses that were explored in this work. Following that we present the methods and results including a cross-sectional study and a longitudinal study. We then discuss the findings.

      Theory and hypotheses

      Motivation types from SDT

      As already noted, SDT uses the autonomy continuum to describe different motivation types. Autonomy refers to endorsing one’s actions at the highest level of reflection (Dworkin, 1988), and the most autonomous end of the continuum is intrinsic motivation, which is defined as the performance of an activity for its inherent satisfaction and enjoyment rather than for separable consequences (Deci and Ryan, 2000). Further, an important aspect of SDT is the proposition that extrinsic motivation can vary in the degree to which it is internalized (Ryan et al., 1985). Ryan (1995) proposed that internalization refers to the transformation by which a regulation that was initially driven by external factors, such as rewards or punishments, becomes internally regulated and, thus, no longer requires the presence of an external contingency. The degree of internalization determines the degree of autonomy or self-determination. According to the degree to which an extrinsic regulation is internalized, the extrinsic motivation can be completely externally regulated, or can be partially or fully internally regulated. Thus, there are different types of extrinsic regulations: external, introjected, and identified (Gagné and Deci, 2005), and the current research relates they types of extrinsic motivation along with intrinsic motivation to various types of work performance.

      External regulation, which refers to performing a task to obtain rewards or avoid punishments, lies at the low end of the continuum; it is the least autonomous form of extrinsic motivation (Gagné et al., 2010). Next is introjected regulation, which refers to a regulation that is internal to the person but has not been accepted as his or her own. Introjected people engage in an activity due to guilt or compulsion or to maintain their self-worth (Koestner and Losier, 2002). Identified regulation refers to performing an activity because one identifies with the personal importance of the behaviour and accepts it as one’s own. With identified regulation, people feel autonomously regulated and greater freedom because the behaviour is more congruent with their personal goals (Gagné and Deci, 2005). For example, if employees strongly value their work training and understand that additional training would be beneficial for their task performance, they would feel relatively autonomous during training, even if the training is not intrinsically interesting.

      Identification differ from intrinsic motivation in that the activity is performed not so much for its own sake (because it is interesting and enjoyable), but for the instrumental value it represents (Gagné et al., 2010). SDT suggests that identified regulation and intrinsic motivation are autonomous forms of motivation that involve the regulation of behaviour with the experiences of volition, psychological freedom, and reflective self-endorsement (Vansteenkiste et al., 2010). In contrast, both external regulation and introjected regulation are controlled forms of motivation involving the regulation of behaviour with the experiences of pressure and are less self-determined. In sum, SDT (Deci and Ryan, 1985; Deci and Ryan, 2000) offers a multidimensional conceptualization of motivation that allows for the assessment of types of motivation.

      The conceptual development of work performance

      Individual work performance has been a critical issue in the field of organization management, and it has attracted extensive attention from academic circles; thus, numerous researchers have focussed on it. However, despite its importance, there is no comprehensive definition or conceptual framework of individual work performance (Koopmans, 2014). Campbell’s (1990) definition of work performance, which is widely endorsed, viewed work performance as “behaviours or actions that are relevant to the goals of the organization”. Regarding the work performance structure, several studies have also tried to identify conceptual frameworks of individual work performance (Koopmans, 2014). Based on conceptual grouping of individual work performance dimensions found in the literature, several broad dimensions could be distinguished: task performance, contextual performance, counterproductive work behaviour, organizational citizenship behaviour, and adaptive performance (Borman and Motowidlo, 1993; Pulakos et al., 2000; Griffin et al., 2007; Rotundo and Sackett, 2002).

      Based on the literature, Wen (2005) constructed a performance rating scale in a Chinese context that examined dimensions of work performance, and concluded that there are four dimensions: task performance, dedicative performance, interpersonal performance, and adaptive performance. The term “task performance” refers to the behaviours that traditional personnel selections focusses on that contributes to the organization’s technical core, and that include job knowledge, work quality, and work skill (Dalal, 2005). Interpersonal performance and dedicative performance have been confirmed as independent components of work performance (Hesketh and Allworth, 1997). This result is different to that of Borman and Motowidlo’s (1993) research which argued that interpersonal performance and dedicative performance are both belong to contextual performance. The term “interpersonal performance” includes interpersonal facilitation and interpersonal collaboration, and “dedicative performance” includes work commitment and difficulty-coping. Wen (2005) concluded that these two independent components are more clear and certain than contextual performance and that, as criteria, they are easy to operationalize and can explain research results. The last term, “adaptive performance”, includes, for example, innovation, autonomous learning, and work adjustment (Allworth and Hesketh, 1999). Thus, compared with the other performance structures, Wen’s four dimensions of work performance represent an empirical extension (Wen et al., 2011), provides more rich and detailed dimensions of employees’ work performance to describe the employees’ behaviours in Chinese contexts, and has been widely used in Chinese contexts (Li et al., 2013; Jian et al., 2012).

      Types of self-regulation and work performance types

      To our knowledge, little research has explored the relationships between the different types of regulation and the work performance dimensions. SDT suggests that employees are intrinsically motivated when they experience pleasure, interest and enjoyment in their jobs (Deci et al., 1989), and these kinds of positive feelings promote their job performance and psychological well-being (Burton et al., 2006). In addition to this theoretical understanding of why intrinsic motivation has a positive impact on employees’ performance, empirical support is also emerging. For example, researchers suggested that intrinsic motivation is positively related to several positive outcomes, such as well-being, work effort, job satisfaction, and affective commitment (Gagné et al., 2010; Dysvik and Kuvaas, 2013). Recently, using common meta-analytic procedures, Cerasoli et al. (2014) also demonstrated that intrinsic motivation was positively related to overall work performance and even more strongly to the “quality” of work performance are positively associated, which means that intrinsic motivation is likely to have a positive effect on employees’ task performance. Besides, according to SDT, those who find a job more intrinsically motivating will expend a higher degree of effort or intensity on the activity (Cerasoli et al., 2014), thus, the level of intrinsic motivation should be linked to dedicative performance through its impact on motivational persistence and efforts (Deci, 1972). Third, intrinsically motivated employees are also more absorbed by their jobs; they may utilize more developmental opportunities to increase their own work effort and may be more involved in the work of their colleagues (Guay et al., 2000), which would promote their interpersonal performance. Empirical research also suggested that the level of effort nurses expended depended on their enjoyment of learning new material and updating skills under complex learning conditions (Simons et al., 2004). Meta-analysis, research revealed that there is a significant positive relation between intrinsic motivation and creativity (de Jesus et al., 2013), and creativity is an instance of adaptive performance, so we expect that intrinsic motivation is positively related to task performance, dedicative performance, interpersonal performance, and adaptive performance.

      As for the relationship between external regulation and work performance, we proposed that external regulation could promote the task performance. Previous research has indicated that incentives do not always undermine intrinsic motivation if the informational functional significance is salient so the incentive would impart a competence-boosting message and, thus, also boost some positive work outcomes (Deci et al., 1999). Technical quality and quantity are two examples of task performance which appears to be fostered by external regulation (Amabile, 1993). An empirical study also suggested that incentives were the deciding (i.e. dominant) predictor of task quantity criteria, which are likely to be noncomplex, repetitive, and require focus and drive for their completion (Cerasoli et al., 2014). Moreover, several scholars have suggested that external controls, incentives, and rewards are necessary to motivate employees’ persistence, performance, and productivity (Steers et al., 2004; Heath, 1999; Shaw and Gupta, 2015), thus we proposed external regulation maybe also related to dedicative performance. In the workplace, we consistently find that extrinsic incentives (e.g. rewards) motivate employees because the direct goal that most employees work is to earn more money. Therefore, we assume that external regulation could also predict dedicative performance.

      SDT propose that introjected and identified regulations are located on the continuum between external regulation and intrinsic motivation in accord with the degree of internalization. SDT suggests that identified regulation, reflects behaviour accompanied by experiences of volition, psychological freedom, and reflective self-endorsement and is an autonomous form of motivation (Vansteenkiste et al., 2010). Introjected regulation is the less internalized form of extrinsic motivation and it is a more controlled form of extrinsic motivation (Vansteenkiste et al., 2010). There are strong distinctions between those different types of self-regulation, and they may each have different relations with some types of work performance.

      We expect that identified regulation is positively related to task performance, dedicative performance, interpersonal performance, and adaptive performance. Dedicative performance and interpersonal performance are the aspects of contextual performance that refer to behaviours that go beyond the formally prescribed work goals and support the organizational, social, and psychological environment in which the technical core must function (Borman and Motowidlo, 1993). Several labels exist for the adaptive performance dimension, including innovation, autonomous learning, and adjustment. Thus, these performance dimensions focus on interpersonal behaviour rather than the outcome, and they are likely to be related to the more self-determined regulation styles. SDT proposes that work climates that promote satisfaction of the three basic psychological needs (needs for autonomy, competence, and relatedness) will enhance employees’ intrinsic motivation and promote the internalization of extrinsic motivation, which in turn will yield important work outcomes (Gagné and Deci, 2005). Besides, studies have indicated that more autonomous (i.e. identified regulation) is associated with greater persistence, performance, social functioning, and physical and psychological wellness (Deci and Ryan, 2000). We, therefore, expect that the well-internalized extrinsic motivations (e.g. identified regulation) have more direct links to those three performance types:

      H1a.

      Intrinsic motivation and identified regulation will be positively related to task performance, dedicative performance, interpersonal performance, and adaptive performance.

      H1b.

      Extrinsic regulation will be positively related to task performance and dedicative performance.

      The effects of motivation changes

      Although research has been conducted on the effects of different types of motivation, little research has focussed on changes in these motivations across a period of time. To our knowledge, when people engage in an activity because they find it interesting, they are intrinsically motivated (Vansteenkiste et al., 2010). Thus, researchers have focussed on specifying the factors in social contexts that produce variability in intrinsic motivation (Ryan and Deci, 2000). Such research could advise managers to implement management interventions to increase people’s intrinsic motivation level over time.

      The most powerful theory that focusses on this view is the cognitive evaluation theory (Deci, 1975), which argues that some external factors (e.g. free choice, communications, and feedback) that induce feelings of autonomy during action can enhance intrinsic motivation for that activity (Zuckerman et al., 1978). It further suggests that feelings of competence are also important, along with feelings of autonomy, for intrinsic motivation. Research has shown that optimally challenging activities (Danner and Lonky, 1981) and positive feedback (Deci, 1971) facilitate intrinsic motivation by promoting a sense of competence when people feel responsible for their performance (Fisher, 1978; Ryan, 1982). In other words, contextual environmental factors can facilitate intrinsic motivation by supporting the basic need satisfactions. Similarly, extrinsically motivated behaviours are not inherently interesting; thus, Ryan et al. (1985) also identified three basic needs (competence, relatedness, and autonomy) as primary for promoting the process of internalization. For example, Ryan et al. (1994) noted that if a child feels related to his or her significant others, he or she will be more likely to internalize the values of the significant others.

      In sum, contextual variables that support the satisfaction of three basic needs would facilitate intrinsic motivation and promote the internalization of extrinsic motivation (Ryan, 1995), which in turn predict people’s work performance and other positive outcomes by influencing their behavioural regulations. Thus, investigating whether motivational changes are related to performance changes, we could demonstrate the benefits of management activity interventions to the extent that supervisors may focus on the contextual variables that promote changes in motivation. Otis et al. (2005) (Reiss, 2005) examined changes in intrinsic and extrinsic motivation during the transition from junior to senior high school as well as the impact of motivational changes on various educational consequences. The results showed that increases in intrinsic motivation and identified regulation were related to positive academic outcomes, but increases in extrinsic and introjected regulation were not related to outcomes. However, little research has focussed on the work motivation changes and their impact on the outcomes. The final aim of our study is to examine the longitudinal markers of motivation change and whether changes in motivation are related to performance changes:

      H2.

      The variation in intrinsic motivation and identified regulation will be positively related to the variation in work performance.

      Study 1

      The major purpose of Study 1 was to profile the relationships among the different types of motivation and work performance of employees. H1 was examined with a cross-sectional method.

      Method

      Participants and procedure

      This study was carried out during the period April 2014 to July in 2014. Participants for this study were 541 employees and their managers from several companies in China, specifically Beijing and Hainan. We select companies mainly in Beijing and Hainan because those two provinces, respectively, represent the North and South places of China, which could reduce the regional differences of our study. We also fully considered the diversity of enterprise property, mainly involving state-owned enterprises, private enterprises, and foreign-capital enterprises, which could have affected on employees’ performance (Jin et al., 2015). Employees filled in the questionnaires to report their demographics and work motivation. Corresponding managers filled in the questionnaires to assess their subordinates’ work performance. There were 415 effective matching responses and the response rate was 77.4 per cent. The per cent of females participants were 33.2 per cent and males were 66.8 per cent; 57.9 per cent had a bachelor’s degree, 9.7 per cent had a master’s degree or above, and 32.4 per cent had a junior college or lower education.

      Measures

      Work motivation. A 12-item work motivation questionnaire – based on the general self-regulation scale (Gagné et al., 2010) – assessed the employees’ present work motivation for external, introjected, identified regulation and intrinsic motivation, with each dimension containing three items. Participants responded to items such as “because I have fun doing this job” on seven-point Likert-type scales. The Cronbach’s α for the total scale was 0.721. For the external motivation, introjected motivation, identified motivation, and intrinsic motivation subscales, the α’s were 0.704, 0.732, 0.748, and 0.780, respectively.

      Work performance. A 29-item work performance questionnaire developed by Wen (2005) assessed the employees’ work performance according to their managers. The scale, consisting of four subscales, concerns the degree of employees’ work performance in each dimension: task performance (seven items), dedicative performance (six items), interpersonal performance (six item), and adaptive performance (ten items). The managers assessed the performance of their subordinates on five-point Likert-type scales. Because dedicative performance and interpersonal performance are somewhat different items, we list the sample items of these two scales. Sample items for dedicative performance scale include “the employee ‘could take on extra responsibilities and extra work’, ‘was able to follow work regulation and job design’, ‘took on protecting the interest and image of my organization’, ‘had a strong sense of pride to his work and his organization’, ‘kept doing his work when facing some setbacks’, ‘kept following work regulation and job design when facing some setbacks.’” Sample items for interpersonal performance scale include “the employee ‘could be friendly in providing TOKEN with good services’, ‘maintain good relationship with colleagues’, ‘maintain good relationship with colleagues from other department’, ‘collaborated with others went well’, ‘provide extra help for colleagues’, ‘actively participated in work activities’”. The Cronbach’s α for the total scale was 0.910. For the task performance, dedicative performance, interpersonal performance, and adaptive performance subscales, the α’s were 0.900, 0.883, 0.905, and 0.937, respectively.

      Control variables: to reduce the likelihood that other variables which may influence work performance would confound the relation examined in this research, we followed other researchers (Griffin et al., 2007; Jian et al., 2012) and controlled for several variables, including gender, education, and company types. As for the company types, it includes the state-owned enterprise, private enterprise and overseas-funded enterprise which may have some impact on employees’ work performance in Chinese context.

The second study was undertaken to extend the findings from the first and to test the hypothesis with a longitudinal pattern and identify the effect of employee motivation changes on work performance over time.

      Study 2

      Study 1 showed that the types of motivation have different links with different performance dimensions. We conducted Study 2 with a longitudinal design to examine the stability of the relationships between motivation and performance.

      Method

      Participants and procedure

      We sampled 165 employees and their managers from several companies in Beijing. At Time 1, employees filled in the questionnaires to measure their work motivation. The corresponding managers filled in the questionnaires to assess their subordinates’ work performance. At Time 2, a month later, the employees and managers filled in the same questionnaires as at Time 1. We choose one month to test changes in motivation and performance because we assumed most employees would have the same jobs for a month later, so there would be relatively little error variance contributed by changes in jobs. There were 139 effective matching responses and the response rate was 85.4 per cent. The per cent of females was 54.0 per cent and males were 56.0 per cent; 43.3 per cent had a bachelor’s degree, 5.0 per cent had a master’s degree or above, and 51.7 per cent had a junior college or lower education.

      Measures

      As in Study 1, work motivation was measured using the questionnaire based on the general self-regulation scale (Gagné et al., 2010) to assess both their initial work motivation and their subsequent work motivation after a month. The scale showed good internal reliability in this study (Cronbach’s α=0.721 at Time 1 and 0.777 at Time 2). Work performance was measured using the work performance questionnaire developed by Wen (2005).This scale also showed excellent internal reliability this time (Cronbach’s α=0.910 at Time 1 and 0.897 at Time 2).

      Results

      First, we used a paired-sample t-test to detect if the variances in motivations and performance were significant between the two measurement points. The results showed that the variances of the four types of motivation were not significant (t (df=139)=−0.43, p>0.05 for extrinsic, t (df=139)=0.361, p>0.05 for introjected, t (df=139)=−1.37, p>0.05 for identified, t (df=139)=−0.25 p>0.05 for intrinsic). The reason for insignificant change is no doubt because the time frame was too short to detect change in people’s regulation. As for performance types, results showed that the variances in task performance (t (df=139)=1.39, p>0.05) and dedicative performance (t (df=139)=−1.27, p>0.05) were not significant. The variances in interpersonal performance (t (df=139)=−2.20, p<0.05) and adaptive performance (t (df=139)=−4.64, p<0.01) were significant.

      Then, we used multiple regression analysis to examine H2 (that variations in motivation would predict variations in work performance).       Overall, the results of Study 2 is interesting to find that an increase in identified regulation would predict an increase in employees’ dedicative and interpersonal performance, while the result did not appear for intrinsic motivation.       Discussion

      The purpose of this study was to examine the detailed relations between motivation and work performance under the frame of SDT and to explore whether well-internalized extrinsic motivation would have a more close relationship with work performance than intrinsic motivation in the workplace. Theory and research suggested that intrinsic motivation was a stronger predictor of outcomes in academics and athletics, among other fields, than external regulation (Deci and Ryan, 2000). We advanced this area of research by exploring the relations from each type of motivation to the types of work performance, especially external regulation, which is considered a core predictor in the workplace. We also extended the findings to test a longitudinal mechanism of change and found that an increase in identified regulation was associated with improvements in work performance.

      Profile of the links between motivation style and work performance types

      Following the SDT perspective, our study examined the links among motivation types and work performance and revealed that well-internalized extrinsic motivation was more important than intrinsic motivation in predicting work performance, thus advancing SDT research in the workplace. We found that when intrinsic motivation and the three types of extrinsic motivation were assessed, only identified regulation had a significant relation to interpersonal performance and adaptive performance. This is because identification keeps employees oriented towards the long-term significance of their current job and may foster persistence in uninteresting but important activities (Burton et al., 2006). Furthermore, it also helps employees identify the organization’s goals and values, and this process could be useful in promoting work performance (Koestner and Losier, 2002). That is, when individuals have internalized the importance and value of their jobs, even if they are not interested in them, they will perform them well because they have accepted their job activities as part of their sense of self (Koestner and Losier, 2002). Subsequently, they are able to guide their behaviour at a high level. Additionally, evidence has also shown that identified regulation ensures the execution of important behaviours that are not considered interesting and that it is more strongly associated with general educational adjustment than intrinsic motivation (Koestner and Losier, 2002).

      Although theory and research have suggested that intrinsic motivation, is the most self-determined form of motivation and is related to many positive outcomes (e.g. persistence, creativity, academic achievement, job satisfaction, and well-being), its effect on work performance is varied (Hardre and Reeve, 2003). This is because intrinsically motivated employees tend to work due to inherent interest, they might not find their jobs interesting, which thus requires some type of extrinsic motivation, and substantial research has showed that well-internalized, autonomous extrinsic motivation is most strongly related to performance (Cerasoli et al., 2014; Deci et al., in press), especially high-quality performance. These findings supports SDT’s assertion that autonomous motivation yields more positive outcomes than controlled motivation (Gagné et al., 2010) and suggests that fully internalized motivation (i.e. identified regulation) which is one type of autonomous motivation, is more effective in promoting performance in some situations than is intrinsic motivation (Burton et al., 2006; Losier and Koestner, 1999).

      Accordingly, based on SDT, these findings highlight the potential importance of identified regulation in the workplace and also solve the conflict between managers’ common knowledge and the common view of SDT. Because identified regulation belongs to autonomous motivation, which is consistently positively related to some work outcomes (Deci and Ryan, 2000), it is also a well-internalized extrinsic motivation. Thus, we suggest that in the workplace, well-internalized extrinsic motivation is a core factor in predicting work performance, and managers should focus on autonomous motivation, and especially the internalized version of it.

      Longitudinal patterns and the mechanisms of change

      A more important contribution of our study is that it tests whether a longitudinal-change model of motivation could apply to employees’ changes (increases or decreases) in performance. Our results show that an increase in identified regulation was the only change that predicted an increase performance, and more specifically the increase in identified regulation predicted increases in dedicative and interpersonal performance. Employing a paired samples t-test, we found that the changes in the four types of regulation were not significant after a month, but we demonstrated that the imperceptible changes in identified regulation predicted the change in performance. These results suggested that identified regulation is a core factor in explaining increases in work performance in the context of an organization. Even a small increase in identified regulation could facilitate work performance; so, we suggest that an intervention focussed on enhancing identified regulation is likely to be important for work performance. As such a future intervention study would be important.

      As noted, this study demonstrated that an increase in a more internalized form of motivation predicted an increase in work performance, whereas intrinsic motivation did not. This is because identification differs from intrinsic motivation in that the work is driven by the value or goals of the task rather than being done for its interest value. That is, even if an employee’s job is not interesting, the employee can be autonomously motivated if he or she internalizes the value or importance of the job. And as shown, this can enhance interpersonal performance and dedicative performance, which are dimensions of contextual performance (Wen, 2005).

      The study samples are all from Chinese companies, and Chinese relationalism is a special and important cultural foundation for nearly all social structures, including the organizational domain, and the idea of guanxi (develop relationships with others, ask colleagues for help) is more common among interpersonal relationships (Hwang, 2000). An increase in a more internalized regulation may motivate employees to make an effort to use their guanxi to improve their technical core functions. These behaviours, in turn, promote interpersonal and dedicative performance. However, further study should replicate these findings in other cultures. Therefore, as advised by SDT, interventions should focus on promoting self-determined motivation.

      From the perspective of SDT, external supports could promote satisfaction of the three basic psychological needs and, in turn, facilitate intrinsic motivation and promote the internalization of extrinsic motivation. An exciting conclusion from the results is that an increase in identified regulation predicted an increase in performance; thus, we suggest that the managerial environment should attempt to provide supports for facilitating the internalization of extrinsic motivations. Employees’ identified regulation would therefore be increased, leading to an increase in interpersonal and dedicative performance. Theory and research have suggested that people who are intrinsically motivated for their jobs have more positive outcomes, but many jobs are not intrinsically interesting, so it is important to promote the internalization of extrinsic motivations (i.e. identified regulation) over time in many work settings.

      Implications for future research

      Our study demonstrates that performance types are predicted by different types of motivation. However, several future studies and limits need to be considered when interpreting the results of the present study. First, we used Wen’s (2005) work performance scales to test Chinese employees’ performance. Although that scale has been widely used in research in Chinese organizational contexts (Jian et al., 2012; Li et al., 2013), an obvious question is whether it is possible to generalize the findings in this study to other countries. Some studies (Pulakos et al., 2000; Griffin et al., 2007) share some similar dimensions with Wen’s (2005) measurement, which means that similar behaviour may occur in certain firms in other countries, but future research in other countries is needed to better understand whether change in identified regulation would also promote performance in those cultures. It would also be important to understand how this measure of performance relates to other indicators of performance used by other researchers.

      Another direction for future research is to test a mediation model of identification and performance types. After a period of time, an increase in identified regulation may lead to increased effort, more persistence and deep absorption, and these factors, in turn, prompts employees to work harder and exhibit better performance. Additionally, we found that the control variable of gender was significant in both Studies 1 and 2; thus, gender could predict performance to some degree. Further research could consider gender as an independent variable rather than a control variable to explore its effect on performance.

      Given that our research examined motivation and performance changes across one month, we assume that employees would have the same tasks for one month, and this ensures that our results would not be interfered with by task differences. While this maybe the reason for insignificant change of motivation. Nevertheless, future research could benefit from a behaviour intervention study, and we expect that motivation types could have obvious changes which affect the outcomes.

      Conclusion

      From the theoretical perspectives and the results of data analyses, we found that identified regulation played a leading role in predicting an increase in work performance types. SDT proposes that as the internalization of work value increases, persistence, positive self-perceptions, and quality of engagement heighten (Deci and Ryan, 2000), thereby leading to better work performance. Thus, it would be important for supervisors to provide a supportive environment that promotes employees’ internalization of the importance of organizational goals, similar to identified self-regulation, which leads individuals to display better work performance.
