      Abstract

      Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) structure determination is an important problem in education, industry, and research. Solving NMR spectra requires expert knowledge, critical thinking, and careful evaluation of multiple features of spectral data. This study explores the capabilities of large language models (LLMs) and large reasoning models (LRMs) for solving NMR spectral tasks. We selected 112 problems from NMR-Challenge.com, which has been used by students practicing NMR structure elucidation, collecting >1 million human responses, and developed a plain text problem format for evaluating LLM reasoning in this domain. We evaluated 10 LLMs (GPT-4o, GPT-4o-mini, o1, o1 mini, o3 mini, Claude-3.5 Sonnet, Gemini 2.0 Flash, Meta Llama 3, ChemDFM-R, and ether0), comparing 5 prompts to spur chain-of-thought reasoning in different ways, especially comparing the influence of providing background NMR chemistry knowledge, reasoning strategy, or both. Newer models trained to emphasize reasoning performed better, and increasing reasoning effort led to modest improvements, but prompting and varying temperature did not have an effect. We also evaluated undergraduate organic chemistry students in a controlled setting and analyzed answer submission statistics from global submissions to NMR-Challenge.com, to characterize human performance on these problems. The top-performing students surpassed smaller models like GPT-4o by 24%, 33%, and 29% on the Easy, Moderate, and Hard sets, respectively. However, reasoning models like o1 exceeded student performance by 13%, 14%, and 19%, respectively. Patterns in mistakes made by humans and LLMs reveal that errors made by LLMs are similar to those typically made by humans, for instance, incorrect positioning of substituents on benzene and incorrect orientation of carboxyl groups in esters. However, LLMs still “think” differently from humans, in some cases, providing answers that no human submitted via the website. This work also illustrates how NMR spectral problems can be used to benchmark LLMs on reasoning-heavy tasks in chemistry, though for this particular set of problems, early 2025 LLMs already exceed undergraduate student performance.

      1. Introduction

      AI-augmented systems have grown significantly with the increasing capabilities of language models. The improvement results from two key factorsscaling model parameters or scaling inference times. The research landscape surrounding foundational language models is rapidly evolving. While larger models generally retain a performance edge, recent work with rstar-math, (1) DeepSeek R1, (2) and others (3,4) shows that smaller models can rival and occasionally exceed the performance of much larger ones. As more powerful models become available at lower costs, especially open-source ones, we can anticipate an increase in their applications. The scientific research community will also leverage these advancements in AI-assisted systems across the fields of chemistry, biology, and materials science.

      A new paradigm that has achieved improvements in model performance on complex tasks is reasoning models. Unlike traditional autoregressive token generation, reasoning models use a sequence of intermediate tokens representing latent reasoning steps. These “chain-of-thought” sequences allow the model to decompose complex tasks before producing a final answer. High-quality reasoning traces are obtained by fine-tuning models on curated reasoning data, so they learn to internalize a structured chain-of-thought and produce a single trajectory of text, or through a search through multiple trajectories, in which the model generates many candidate reasoning paths and uses trial-and-error, rejection sampling, or verifier-guided selection to identify correct trajectories. (5)

      The early demonstration of this approach is seen in the basic Chain of Thought (CoT) prompting technique. (6,7) Since then, effective strategies have been outlined in the literature, including reinforcement learning, (2) supervised fine-tuning, (3) and preference modeling. (1) More broadly, emerging work on LLM-based agentic systems extends these ideas to more structured forms of reasoning, including planning, tool use, search, feedback, and collaborative inference across multiple tasks. (8−12)

      Most studies on the reasoning capabilities of LLMs focus on their abilities to solve problems in mathematics or planning. For instance, when employing in-context learning for mathematical problems like division, we present the model with several input–output examples, and the observed improvements are attributed to the model’s inductive reasoning abilities, but the problem itself more closely aligns with deductive reasoning. (13) This overlap of prompting techniques and problem types makes it difficult to identify areas where LLMs need improvement in their reasoning abilities. This work provides benchmark data for complex, nonlinear problem-solving in organic chemistry, while also addressing a practical challenge: identifying chemical structures that can explain observations from nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy experiments.

      NMR uses oscillating magnetic fields to analyze molecule structures from their chemical shifts. (14,15) Atoms resonate at different frequencies based on their local unique environments, and the resulting NMR spectrum provides insights into interactions among specific atoms within a molecule, ultimately deducing the molecule’s whole structure. NMR spectroscopy is an example of abductive reasoning used in scientific problem-solving. As this process involves using observations (NMR spectrum) to infer the most plausible explanation for a phenomenon (molecule identity), it is a form of “inference to the best explanation” where the goal is to identify the hypothesis that most likely explains the observed data. (16) Solving NMR spectral tasks “increases students’ critical thinking abilities because they have to evaluate multiple aspects at the same time, such as the number, intensity, and shape of signals, chemical shifts, and J-coupling values”, (17) making these tasks a mainstay in undergraduate training in organic chemistry.

      Modern tools for NMR structure determination most commonly include methods based on deep neural networks (18−26) and Transformers. (27−31) These approaches frame the problem as a supervised learning task, with spectra as features and molecular assignments as labels. After the model has been trained, inferences about new spectra can be obtained as model predictions. However, such methods typically rely on black-box architectures and hence do not offer explanations for their results. They also do not handle corner cases and instances not encountered during training well and require curated, large-scale labeled data sets, which are expensive to assemble for training. Automated tools that use formal logic have also been explored to identify molecules from spectral data; (32−35) however, they demand considerable effort from human experts to define the rules of the system accurately.

      Nevertheless, these approaches do not explicitly isolate reasoning from experimental spectral data, leaving a gap in the evaluation of such capabilities. We recognize solving an NMR spectral problem as fundamentally a multistep reasoning task (36) and aim to explore the reasoning capabilities of LLMs. At its core, elucidating the chemical structure from NMR data is an abductive reasoning task, (37,38) in which hypothesis formation is paired with deduction, to reason from the spectra to a plausible chemical structure. Previous studies have focused on predicting observable signals from SMILES (Simplified Molecular Input Line Entry System) strings or molecular structures, (39) or on extracting the NMR data itself, (40) while this work highlights the reasoning capabilities of LLMs by providing the necessary data for predicting the molecule. The concept was developed during our 2024 LLM Hackathon for Applications in Materials and Chemistry project, where we manually curated NMR data sets to test our approach. (41) We found GPT-4 to be successful in some cases; however, the scratchpad showed interesting patterns of (apparent) reasoning in its outputs. Building on this preliminary work, this paper presents an improved benchmark data set and a systematic exploration of prompting strategies, temperatures, reasoning efforts, and models in identifying molecules from NMR spectra.

      This work focuses on a single task in chemistry to evaluate the performance of LLMs, in contrast to other benchmarks that combine various subfields (42−44) or tasks together. (39,45) Similar to the recently proposed ChemIQ benchmark, (46) our prompting approach is open-ended rather than employing multiple-choice formats. However, we specifically instruct the LLM to identify chemicals from spectral data by their names and then convert the answer to SMILES ourselves during the grading process. In contrast, ChemIQ requires the generation of SMILES strings from NMR data, which introduces an additional reasoning component. Although an LLM may accurately identify the name of a molecule, it could still produce an incorrect SMILES string, making it difficult to identify its reasoning errors. Converting a molecule name to a SMILES string representation involves additional reasoning, which could be explored in future research.

      2. Method

      2.1. Data Processing

      We used the NMR spectral data that were used to create NMR-Challenge.com, an interactive website featuring exercises categorized into Easy, Moderate, and Hard levels. (17,47) For each problem, the website provides images of 1D spectra (1H, 13C, 19F) at the basic level and additionally 2D spectra (COSY, HSQC, HMBC) at the advanced level, along with the corresponding chemical formula. We used MNOVA (48) multiplet analysis to extract and summarize numerical data about peaks. The multiplet analysis compresses the raw spectral data, making the input text more tractable for the LLMs, which was not done in the hackathon version of our project. (41) This also prevents direct feeding of website data to the LLMs, reducing the risk of triggering memorization. For humans with access to the visual spectrum, peak-picking may be useful but not likely as critical. Our data processing pipeline formatted these data in JavaScript Object Notation (JSON) format, where each entry contains molecular formula (if applicable), a unique problem ID, and an array of information about each peak: label with multiplicity, chemical shift, integration range, hydrogen count, integral value, multiplicity class, and J-coupling values as shown in Figure 1. Of the 148 Basic problems, we focused our analysis on problems with 1D 1H and 13C NMR spectra, excluding 19F-containing problems, as well as excluding those that did not have valid .mnova files suitable for our pipeline: 53 Easy, 35 Moderate, and 24 Hard problems, for a total of 112 problems.

However, without the formula, the normalized values provide only relative ratios without specific hydrogen counts.       2.2. Experimental Details

      We evaluated 10 large language models (LLMs) on the NMR benchmark, comprising both proprietary and open-weight systems. For closed-source models, we selected GPT-4o, GPT-4o mini, o1, o1 mini, and o3 mini from OpenAI; (49) Claude-3.5 Sonnet from Anthropic; (50) and Gemini 2.0 Flash from Google DeepMind. (51) Among these, the OpenAI o-series and Gemini are described as reasoning models that employ scaled test-time computation. (52) For closed-source models, we evaluated Meta-Llama-3–70B by Meta, (53) ChemDFM-R-14B by OpenDFM, (54) and ether0 by FutureHouse. (55) ChemDFM-R-14B is a domain-specialized model trained on extensive chemical literature, reactions, and molecular properties, and it was not explicitly trained on NMR analysis tasks.

      We varied the model temperature for the closed-source models to assess the effect of deterministic versus stochastic token sampling. The open-source models, however, were evaluated using 16-bit quantization and fixed optimal performance setup parameters as defined in their respective documentation. We did not vary the temperature for the open-source models, as preliminary tests indicated that stochastic sampling produced erratic and largely incoherent outputs, likely due to limited instruction tuning.

      Each model was tested with five prompt styles to establish baseline performance and evaluated with and without chemical formulas in the spectral information. The prompts were designed to assess how varying levels of guidance influence the reasoning process and overall model performance. In particular, we considered the domain of NMR structure elucidation as requiring domain-specific knowledge as well as domain-specific problem-solving strategy. For instance, the characteristic shifts, peak intensities, and splitting patterns associated with important functional groups would be learned knowledge. In contrast, “iteratively hypothesize fragments before synthesizing a whole structure (and repeat until the solution is consistent)” would be a logical strategy.

      P1 Base Prompt: This prompt provides minimal user input, allowing for nonguided generation. It was used as a control condition to observe the inherent reasoning abilities of LLMs for solving the NMR spectra problems. P2 Standard CoT prompt: This prompt uses the standard CoT prompt introduced by Wei et al., (7) which encourages step-by-step reasoning to solve complex problems. P3 CoT prompt with logical tips: Building on the standard CoT, this prompt variation includes domain-specific logical tips. It encourages the model to identify fragments, rank hypotheses, verify stoichiometry, and propose plausible molecular fragments while ensuring consistency in connectivity. P4 CoT prompt with knowledge: A human expert on NMR data interpretation provided approximately 1 page of guidance on domain-specific knowledge involved in solving NMR spectra, for instance, the characteristic shifts, peak intensities, and splitting patterns associated with important functional groups. P5 CoT prompt with knowledge and logical tips This is the most comprehensive of our modalities and combines the CoT prompt with logical tips and human-expert insights.

      The complete code for all prompt templates and representative prompt-response examples is available in our GitHub repository: https://github.com/ATOMSLab/LLM-NMR.

      2.3. Evaluation Metrics

      To evaluate how well large language models (LLMs) predict molecular structures from NMR spectral data, we needed a reliable method to compare their output to the correct answer, given that a molecule can have many different names, and we could not anticipate which name the LLM would generate. We retrieved Simplified Molecular Input Line Entry System, i.e., SMILES (56) representations of chemical names for the correct answer and the LLM’s response using PubChemPy, (57) a Python wrapper for the PubChem database. (58) When PubChem lookup failed, we used OPSIN (Open Parser for Systematic IUPAC Nomenclature) (59) as a fallback.

      We graded the prediction of the models using multiple criteria: compound name matching, SMILES string match, and Tanimoto similarity using rdFingerprintGenerator in RDKit (an open-source cheminformatics toolkit). (60) A prediction received a score of 1 if any of the following conditions were met: (1) the predicted compound name matched the ground truth name; (2) the predicted and ground truth SMILES matched exactly; and (3) the Tanimoto similarity is 1, confirming that the molecules were truly identical. All models produced responses with varying degrees of adherence to the template provided in the context. Consequently, different functions were written to extract the names of the molecules, with some answers requiring manual extraction. We did not penalize models in assessing their chemical reasoning abilities if they failed to follow the specified output template. However, we observed that models that scored higher in NMR structure determination also tended to follow the instructions correctly more often.

      3. Results and Discussion

      We organized our findings into three sections. First, we analyze the performance of all models under varying temperatures, prompting styles, and reasoning levels in solving NMR spectral problems. Next, we show the results of human performance on the same problems. Finally, we present additional experimental results where we align LLM conditions more closely with those used in human testing.

      3.1. Tuning Prompts, Temperature, and Reasoning Depths of LLMs and LRMs

      3.1.1. Effect of Prompting Style

      Easy problems consistently scored the highest in both experiment settings, with and without the formula included. Moderate and Hard tasks have similar accuracy when formula is included. However, there is a significant drop in performance for Hard tasks, which fell well below Moderate tasks when no formula is included.

      We hypothesized that incorporating domain knowledge and logical tips into our prompts would lead to systematic improvements in the model performance. However, in practice, neither LLMs nor LRMs showed any benefit from these advanced prompt templates (see Figure 3). In fact, we sometimes observed a decline in scores, for example, Gemini 2.0 Flash score dropped from P4 to P5 on Easy problems (with formula), and Claude Sonnet 3.5 score fell from P1 to P2 on Moderate problems (with formula). The changes are minimal for most models, with some modest improvements; however, no consistent upward or downward trend is observed, contrary to our expectations. OpenAI’s guide to using reasoning models dictates that these models perform better on tasks with high-level guidance alone and that CoT-type prompts may impede performance, which seems to be observed in some of the cases here. (61)

      3.1.2. Effect of Temperature

      We tested the effect of temperature on four models: GPT-4o, GPT-4o mini, Claude 3.5 Sonnet, and Gemini 2.0 Flash. The other OpenAI models (o1 mini, o3 mini, and o1) have their temperature fixed at 1 as part of model design.

      In all panels of Figure 4, we see no consistent effect of temperature across model type or difficulty level. No model significantly outperforms or underperforms at any given temperature. However, the average performance consistently follows the order from Easy to Moderate to Hard across all models. Notably, including the formula significantly improves accuracy: the “with formula” conditions (top row) consistently achieve much higher scores than their “without formula” counterparts (bottom row). These findings are also consistent across other prompt types (see Figure S1 in SI). Another study (62) also noted that if a model performs reasonably well at problem-solving, temperature values between 0 and 1 do not significantly impact the results.

      At its core, temperature impacts the softmax function used to generate output probabilities that create response tokens. Lower temperatures sharpen the distribution, making the model more deterministic. Higher temperatures lead to more randomness and a uniform output distribution. This randomness can foster creativity for certain tasks, but it can also result in inaccuracies due to unpredictability. Additionally, we cannot pinpoint the effects of temperature across models as they use different search algorithms, which may or may not be influenced by temperature. For example, a model using beam search behaves very differently from one using greedy search. (63) We anticipated that a higher temperature would encourage creative hypothesis formation and hinder precise reasoning, potentially leading to a counterbalancing effect. However, we did not observe significant effects in any case.

      3.1.3. Effect of Reasoning Effort

      Reasoning models provide both a response and a CoT, allowing them to learn not just “what” to answer but also “how” to answer. This improvement in performance, as they generate more tokens, is called inference-time scaling. This is achieved in various ways, such as selecting the best answer after sampling many generations (output-focused) or modifying the proposal distribution (input-focused) by assigning more weight to reasoning tokens.

      The recent OpenAI reasoning models provide fine-tuning through reasoning effort tuning as a hyperparameter. The reasoning_effort parameter has three settings: low, medium, and high. Low prioritizes speed and uses fewer tokens; high enables detailed reasoning. The default setting is medium, which balances speed and accuracy. We tested the o3 mini model with varying levels of “reasoning effort” to observe how it reasons and performs across the NMR problem categories. Across all prompt styles, higher reasoning effort leads to better scores (see Figure 5) as expected. In the “with formula” condition, the gap between low, medium, and high effort is wider for Moderate and Hard problems. Conversely, the “without formula” condition shows a narrower gap with difficulty, and overall lower scores. We can apply high reasoning effort to difficult problems (Moderate/Hard, “with formula”) or to problems with unknown domains (Easy, “without formula”).

      3.3. Assessing Student Performance

      The study involved 140 undergraduate students enrolled in the organic chemistry laboratory course at the University of Maryland, Baltimore County (UMBC). Before participating in this research, the students had covered the topic of NMR spectroscopy and had approximately two months of experience with it. Each student was assigned six problems categorized as Easy, three as Moderate, and two as Hard, all delivered through the interactive testing environment on the NMR-Challenge.com website. (17,47) Additionally, they were given six optional problems to solve, with two from each category. While they could take as long as necessary, they were encouraged to spend around 15 min on each problem.

      NMR-Challenge.com provides molecular formulas, as well as images of both 13C NMR and 1H NMR spectra with annotated peak information, and tasks students with drawing a chemical structure in the app. Submitted answers are graded immediately, with additional feedback provided if the submitted answer had the wrong formula. To identify the top quartile, we grouped participants by “Participant ID” and counted the number of NMR problems each attempted and the number they answered correctly. We then calculated an individual accuracy rate by dividing correct answers by total attempts. We set the 75th percentile of these accuracy rates as the cutoff for the top quartile. 39 of the 140 participants met this threshold and were labeled top quartile, shown in the plot on the right. Figure 7 illustrates the performance of students, and Section S1.2 in SI shows the distribution of problems attempted by students across NMR problem difficulty levels.

      Since NMR-Challenge.com (17,47) provides molecular formulas as well as images of both 13C NMR and 1H NMR spectra, we conducted an additional experiment with the LLMs. The main difference from our previous experimental runs was the inclusion of 13C NMR data alongside un-normalized 1H NMR data (formatted in JSON as illustrated in Figure 1), so that human participants and LLMs received the same information. That said, the human participants had access to visual representations of the spectra, while the LLMs were provided with a text input after multiplet peak analysis. While researchers have explored multimodal approaches and visual language models for interpreting NMR spectra, (26,64−66) our work emphasizes reasoning capabilities, which are better evaluated when decoupled from perception-related issues associated with figure interpretation.       The performance trends for the models are consistent with our previous runs, with o1 leading with an accuracy of 81%. o3 mini follows at 73%, and then Claude 3.5 Sonnet at 61%. Gemini 2.0 Flash scores around 43%, similar to its performance in the previous run under similar conditions. o1 mini and GPT-4o score about 30%, while GPT-4o mini has the lowest performance at ∼14%.       In the right panel of Figure 8B, we categorically compare the performance of students with the leading reasoning and nonreasoning language models. The results show that all students outperform GPT-4o (LLM); however, o1 (LRM) outperforms even top students across all problem categories. We further analyzed the performance using generalized linear mixed-effects models (GLMMs), which are well-suited for binary outcome data with hierarchical structure. (67−69) Our data has two key features: (1) the outcome is binary (either correct/incorrect), requiring logistic regression, and (2) responses are clustered by problem, as the same problems were answered by multiple solvers (students and LLMs), violating the independence assumption of standard regression. GLMMs address this by incorporating random effects that account for the correlation among responses to the same problem. Similar approaches have been used in recent work comparing LLM and human performance on cognitive and reasoning tasks, (70,71) on medical examinations, (72) and in evaluating LLM outputs against human-authored scientific texts. (73)

      We fit GLMMs with a binomial distribution and a logit link using the lme4 package in R. (74) The outcome was binary (correct/incorrect), with fixed effects including solver type and problem difficulty (Easy, Moderate, Hard), with Easy as the reference category for difficulty and students for solver type. Random intercepts were included for the problem (n = 112) and solver (n = 175; 140 students, 20 GPT-4o configurations, 15 o1 configurations). We conducted two analyses: one comparing all students (n = 140) to LLMs and another comparing top-performing students (n = 39) to LLMs. Detailed results from the GLMM models are shown in Table S1 in the SI. We compare odds ratios (ORs), which indicate the relative odds of a correct response compared to the reference group, where OR > 1 indicates better performance and OR < 1 indicates worse performance. In our first analysis across all students, GPT-4o did not differ significantly from students (OR = 0.99, p = 0.974), whereas o1 significantly outperformed students (OR = 58.79, p < 0.001). In our second analysis, which included top students, GPT-4o performed significantly worse than top students (OR = 0.08, p < 0.001), whereas o1 significantly outperformed top students (OR = 7.48, p < 0.001). Accuracy declined with problem difficulty in both analyses (all p-values <0.001).

      These findings do not detract from NMR’s special position in chemical education, and we recommend that instructors continue to teach and assign NMR spectral tasks. NMR spectral tasks have long been a mainstay of chemical education, specifically because they cultivate critical thinking alongside an understanding of molecular structure and characterization. After all, arithmetic has been essential for math education, both before and after calculators could perform this task more easily and accurately than humans. The presence of new AI tools for solving chemistry problems does not necessarily change what problems are best for building up human skills.

      3.5. Evaluating Model Reasoning

      We tested whether models relied on memorized problem–answer pairs from NMR-Challenge.com rather than reasoning over spectral data using two evaluations. First, we queried models with prompts like: “What is the solution to problem ID X from NMR-Challenge.com?” No model returned a molecular structure, suggesting that the models do not directly recall problem–answer pairs indexed by website identifiers. To further reduce the possibility of answer lookup by both humans and models, problem IDs were randomized prior to our evaluation. Second, we tested GPT-4o (top nonreasoning) and o1 (top reasoning) models using only the molecular formula, and no spectral data. In this setting, GPT-4o and o1 achieved 100% accuracy in three trials on 18 and 28 of the 112 problems, respectively. Performance comparisons with students on this same subset are shown in Figure S4 in the SI. Five problems were solved perfectly by both students and models; these corresponded to chemically prevalent cases, such as simple alkanes (pentane), common aromatics (benzene, toluene, and chlorobenzene), and small alcohols (methanol). For methanol, only one constitutional isomer is possible; no NMR-specific reasoning is needed. Alternative isomers are technically possible for the others (for instance, C6H6could be 1,5-hexadiyne, instead of benzene), but the common isomer was guessed every time, because the problem was very easy to guess and/or to reason through. However, beyond these examples, student and LLM performance diverged, with human participants submitting more diverse hypotheses, and with LLMs generally maintaining their first-guess performance. For C6H8O7, both GPT-4o and o1 always guessed citric acid when given the formula only, and also got this correct in all NMR problems, as wellthis proved much more difficult for humansonly 41% of website submissions had the correct structure. This illustrates that these models have memorized salient name/formula associations, which improved performance on some problems. However, this did not always guarantee high performance. The polycyclic aromatic acridine (C13H9N) has few common isomers, and GPT-4o and o1 strongly associated acridine with this formula. However, when provided with the formula and the spectra, GPT-4o and o1 “reasoned” to an incorrect guess 75% and 16% of the time.

      Furthermore, we manually analyzed the reasoning quality in about 190 LLM scratchpads to more deeply assess the spectral interpretation of the models. Traces were sampled across six experimental conditions: 2 models (o1 vs GPT-4o) × 2 prompt types (P1 vs P5) × 2 reasoning levels for o1 (high vs low). For each condition, we selected approximately 12 problems per difficulty level (Easy, Moderate, Hard), balancing correct and incorrect responses (6 of each), generating roughly 36 problems per condition. Due to performance differences, GPT-4o had fewer correct responses available for sampling on Hard problems, whereas o1 had fewer incorrect responses on Easy problems.

      Analysis of reasoning traces revealed several systematic shortcomings in models’ interpretation of NMR spectra. A recurring issue was the failure to correctly recognize substitution patterns in aromatic systems, particularly disubstituted benzene rings, as reflected in both 1H splitting patterns and the expected number of 13C signals. LLMs struggled to identify exchangeable proton signals, and they often overlooked key spectral clues, leading to decisions based on incomplete or inconsistent assignments.

      For instance, we observed a misinterpretation of exchangeable proton signals in problem ID # 21 (prompt P5, o1, low reasoning). The correct structure, ethylene glycolate, was ultimately identified by the LLM; however, it misinterpreted the OH proton signal at 2.17 ppm during its reasoning process. In the scratchpad, the model stated: “However, because there are no carbons in the ≈20–40 ppm region in 13C, the 2.17 ppm signal is likely an unusual splitting pattern or impurity/complex coupling and must correspond to a CH2, also ultimately associated with an oxygen (consistent with the ≈60 ppm carbon).” This reflects a fundamental misunderstanding of the behavior of exchangeable protons in NMR spectra. We also observed misinterpretation of benzene substitution in Problem ID # 137 (prompt P5, o1, low reasoning). Here, the LLM correctly identified the substituentsan aldehyde group and an ethoxy groupbut incorrectly assigned them to para positions, whereas the true substitution pattern is ortho. An ortho-disubstituted benzene ring should exhibit six aromatic carbon signals and four aromatic proton signals, in contrast to the four carbon and two proton signals expected for a para-disubstituted ring. Nevertheless, the scratchpad asserted: “Four aromatic protons appear between 7.0 and 8.0 ppm with splitting patterns suggesting a para-disubstituted ring (two sets of doublets/meta couplings).” This illustrates a systematic error in the recognition of aromatic substitution patterns. Strikingly, the LLMs’ errors in interpreting exchangeable proton signals and benzene substitution patterns parallel the difficulties routinely encountered by human students analyzing NMR spectra. (17)

      During the decision-making process, models also frequently lost track of earlier reasoning steps or were able to reason locally about each peak group but failed to correctly synthesize a consistent structure. This resulted in cases where individual peak assignments were correct, but the final structural conclusion was incorrect. This prevalence of “Wrong Answer, Sound Reasoning” was particularly pronounced in GPT-4o (25% of cases) compared to o1 (7%), suggesting that o1 is more reliable in maintaining consistency between intermediate steps and conclusions (see Figure 9).

      3.6. Error Pattern Analysis of LLMs and Humans

      Both humans and LLMs must generate and navigate an implicit “space” of possible structural hypotheses to evaluate and rank. We presume that when a human or an LLM produces a formula-consistent yet inaccurate answer, this reveals characteristics of the hypothesis spaces generated and navigated by humans and LLMs. If an LLM’s answer is provided in a direct response (no chain of thought), we could enumerate possible answers and measure the log probabilities of the tokens to decipher the model’s relative ranking of hypotheses. However, the scratchpad renders this approach invalid, and we also do not have access to what the humans were thinking; so we instead analyze the distribution of right and wrong answers as indicative of this distribution of hypotheses.

      The most common mistakes made by language models in comparison to human participants across all 112 benchmark problems are listed in SI Section S1.5, with a subset illustrated in Figure 10. The human participant data for comparison is from global submissions to NMR-Challenge.com, since it has a large database (of over 1 million submissions) and is more statistically significant. In contrast, the study involving UMBC students in Section 3.3, while gathering many fewer responses, provides a controlled environment for assessing accuracy, since website submissions allow humans multiple tries. We extracted the most common mistakes made by language models by combining the answers from all experiments conducted with the o1 and GPT-4o models in Section 3.4. For o1 model, each problem has 5 (prompt variations) × 3 (reasoning effort) possible answers, and GPT-4o model has 5 (prompt variations) × 4 (temperature variations) possible answers.

      In our opinion, one of the most fundamental reasoning aspects of these problems (when the formula is provided) is ensuring that the hypothesized molecule has the correct formula. By simply checking the formula (and not giving up!), one can be certain to prevent wrong answers of this kind. Yet for GPT-4o, this was the most common failure mode: 57 out of 112 problems had molecules with an incorrect formula as the most popular wrong answer. This revealed an astounding inability of GPT-4o to perform in-context reasoning about the most basic aspect of its answer, the formula. In comparison, the o1 model showed a markedly lower incidence of incorrect molecular formulas (6 out of 112) in its most common erroneous output. This is much better, but far from perfect reasoning behavior: keep in mind that we specifically prompted these models to “be mindful of stoichiometry and ensure consistency with the given formula”, and models failed to follow this instruction if they did not continue to perform this check until they generated a consistent guess. NMR-Challenge.com records answer submissions, but does not store those with incorrect formulas, so this particular error type could not be directly compared to human submissions.

      The NMR-Challenge.com website data does allow us to evaluate more advanced errors, arising when the formula is correct, yet the answer is incorrect. Comparing these errors from LLMs and human participants reveals overlapping reasoning patterns and shared failure modes. Across the benchmark set of 112 problems, GPT-4o scored 100% in 17 cases (14 classified as Easy and 3 as Moderate), never generating an incorrect answer for us to consider. For these same tasks, human participants on the NMR-Challenge.com platform achieved success rates ranging from 41% to 100%, with an average of 80%. In contrast, GPT-4o scored 0% on 34 tasks (8 Easy, 13 Moderate, and 13 Hard). Human performance on these tasks was also significantly lower, with success rates between 8% and 64% (mean: 39%). Among the 38 tasks, GPT-4o did not succeed in 100% of the trials but generated structures with correct formulas, revealing further insight into the model’s behavior. In 8 of these cases, the GPT-4o output matched the most frequently submitted incorrect structure by human users. An additional 6 and 12 cases matched the second and third-to-fifth most common incorrect human submissions, respectively. In 10 cases, GPT-4o’s proposed structure was observed less frequently among human responses, and in 2 cases, GPT-4o’s answer was unique, and never submitted to NMR-Challenge.com. In contrast, o1 scored 100% in 68 tasks (43 Easy, 17 Moderate, 8 Hard), for which the average human success rate was 57%. It failed entirely on 5 tasks (1 Easy, 1 Moderate, 3 Hard), scoring 0%, where the average human success rate was 31%. Among tasks not consistently solved by o1, only 6 featured incorrect molecular formulas in its most common erroneous output. For o1’s incorrect but formula-valid predictions (in 38 tasks), alignment with human errors was again notable: 13 matched the most common incorrect human structure, 3 matched the second most common, and 8 matched the third-to-fifth most common. In 11 cases, o1’s outputs were less frequently observed among human submissions. One of the incorrect structures was not observed in the human data set, and two were chemically invalid due to violations of basic valency rules (e.g., pentavalent carbon, trivalent oxygen). However, we cannot compare these to invalid molecule responses from humans, as the NMR-Challenge website provides strong feedback via the drawing tool, which is not provided in the LLM’s text-based environment. All incorrect structures proposed by the models are detailed in S1.5.

      The most common structural errors made by the LLMs share many features with the most frequent incorrect submissions by human participants on NMR-Challenge.com. (75) Previous work has characterized the common failure modes of humans in these problems; (17) we find that LLMs likewise suffer from similar failure modes, especially misassigning the positions of substituents on aromatic rings and incorrectly orienting ester functional groups. These recurring mistakes highlight specific challenges in interpreting NMR spectra that are common to both humans and machine reasoning. These results point to two possible conclusions about the nature of human and LLM reasoning in NMR structure determination. First, they may suggest behavioral similarity in error patterns between LLMs and humans when interpreting NMR data, especially in the nature and distribution of incorrect structural hypotheses. However, even if LLMs and humans reason rather differently about these problems (such as when incorrect LLM-generated answers were never proposed by humans), their answer distributions can still look similar when a handful of high-probability hypotheses dominate, while rare alternatives remain hidden with standard sampling.

      3.7. Suitability of NMR-Challenge.com as an LLM Benchmark

      Our benchmark includes 112 problems with experimental spectra, for which human performance data are available. With o1 already achieving 60% accuracy on Hard problems and outperforming top students (Figure 8), this suggests that our benchmark is already or nearly saturated by existing LLMs (though they have some room for improvement). Generating synthetic NMR spectra of increasing difficulty could be considered next, although one would need to be careful in the problem design to avoid indeterminacy. We aimed to focus this benchmark on reasoning and nonlinear problem-solving capabilities, and thus, our preprocessing pipeline (multiplet analysis) transformed the raw spectra into a more abstract representation. If more than one structure could be a faithful explanation of the collection of multiplets, then grading would become problematic. The current data set avoids ambiguity, having been carefully designed by human educators to have a single correct answer.

      In terms of practical implications, we think these frontier chatbot models would not be the best choice for automating NMR structure determination in laboratory settingsthat task would be better handled by specialized ML systems. Specifically purpose-built transformer models trained on millions of synthetic spectra (28,30,64) would almost certainly excel on our benchmark; such tools are reported to demonstrate effectiveness on molecules with 19 heavy atoms and trillions of isomers (30) (our data set does include a few molecules with up to 23 heavy atoms (ID 174), but these have substantial symmetry that makes them still tractable for human problem-solving). Such models might exploit subtle signal patterns in their large training data sets that would not be reflected in human reasoning. General-purpose AI can and should be capable of understanding molecular structure through chemical reasoning, but do not necessarily need to be equipped for such intense tasks. We found anecdotally that chatbots can address highly related tasks, such as “What parts of this molecule correspond to this peak?” Such questions cannot even be posed to systems that take raw spectra as input and output SMILES. Just as a chatbot should ideally be able to add 2-digit numbers, while switching to a calculator for evaluating 8-digit multiplication, a chemical reasoning model should ideally know when to switch from chain of thought reasoning in a domain it understands, to calling domain-specific tools (like a fine-tuned ML model for raw spectra-to-SMILES) when faced with a scenario in which reasoning is unlikely to be sufficient.

      4. Conclusions

      This work is an initial reasoning-focused LLM benchmark for evaluating chemical problem-solving in the NMR domain, in which chemistry knowledge is intertwined with complex, multistep reasoning. The study demonstrates that language models can interpret chemical structures from NMR spectroscopy data, with older, nonreasoning models like GPT-4o demonstrating significantly poorer performance than more recent reasoning-focused models like o1. o1 outperformed top undergraduate students in these tasks, but is not a perfect reasoner, continuing to make mistakes such as answering with a molecule whose formula is inconsistent with that given in the prompt. Language models implicitly use chain-of-thought reasoning, and neither the temperature nor prompt engineering consistently affected their performance. Humans and LLMs generally found the same sets of problems to be easy and hard and share common failure modes (e.g., incorrectly orienting ester functional groups), yet some LLM-generated answers had never been guessed by humans.
