Abstract

This study suggests an integrated model that explains the associations among motivations for using cellphone texting, usage patterns, and psychological consequences. Using data from an online survey (N = 335), the study identified motivations of communication with strong ties and weak ties, which were found to be associated with different usage patterns of cellphone texting. Further, time spent on cellphone texting was negatively associated with relationship satisfaction, while the number of text messages sent and received was associated with reduced feelings of loneliness through higher levels of perceived intimacy and relationship satisfaction.

1. Introduction

Cellphone texting has become one of the primary communication activities for relationship maintenance in recent years. As of May 2013, 81% of cellphone owners reported sending or receiving text messages, and this proportion reached up to 94–97% for younger adults aged between 18 and 49 (Duggan, 2013). Teens who texted often were more likely to own a smartphone as opposed to a regular cellphone compared to those who texted less often (Pew Research Center, 2012). Given that 83% of people in the age group of 18–29 own a smartphone as of January 2014 (Pew Research Center, 2014), which provides an easy and convenient interface for typing, the use of texting can be expected to continue to be a prevalent communication activity.
In accordance with the widespread use of cellphone texting, an ample body of research on cellphone texting and its social and psychological consequences has been accumulated. For instance, early this century, Palen (2002) suggested that mobile technology would revolutionize the ways in which people maintain their social networks. As such, Hall and Baym (2011) found that using mobile phone—including both calling and texting—in close relationships enhanced expectations of relationship maintenance, which in turn increased relationship satisfaction. Pierce (2009) discovered that cellphone texting played an essential role in maintaining social contacts among adolescents who suffered from various forms of social anxiety. In another vein, numerous studies have investigated the motivations for using cellphone texting. For example, Reid and Reid (2007) found that anxious people preferred texting to calling in communicating with expressive and intimate contacts. Cacioppo et al. (2006) demonstrated that shy people used text messaging more than non-shy people in order to meet their need for meaningful social interaction. In the realm of the uses and gratifications (U&G) approach (Katz, Blumler, & Gurevitch, 1974), Leung (2007) discovered that college students used texting with the motivations of entertainment, affection, fashion, escape, convenient and low cost, and coordination. Similarly, Jin and Park (2010) showed that interpersonal motives such as affection and inclusion led to frequent uses of cellphone texting. Further, Grellhesl and Punyanunt-Carter (2012) found that there were sex differences among specific motivations for cellphone texting.
Although these studies uncovered various ways in which cellphone texting is embedded in and affects our communication practices, the links among motivations, usage patterns, and psychological outcomes of cellphone texting have been understudied. These variables have been largely examined in isolation, typically within the U&G framework or the media effects paradigm. Upon this understanding, the current study follows Park and Lee's (2014) claim that these three components—motivations, usage, and psychological outcomes—are interrelated and thus could reveal a deeper understanding of media use when examined in an integrative manner. Therefore, the main goal of the current study is to comprehend how motivations for using cellphone texting are associated with usage patterns, and how the usage patterns, in turn, influence psychological outcomes.
In the present study, three psychological consequences are examined: intimacy, relationship satisfaction, and loneliness. Intimacy has been widely studied as a consequence of interpersonal communication via communication technologies (e.g., Hian et al., 2004, Park et al., 2011). Relationship satisfaction, which has been documented particularly in the context of romantic relationships (e.g., Anderson and Emmers-Sommer, 2006, Meeks et al., 1998), can be expected to exert influence on general satisfaction in relationships between communication partners as well. Lastly, a significant number of studies have investigated the effect of using communication technologies or media on loneliness (e.g., Jin and Park, 2013, Sum et al., 2008). Given that cellphone use, including texting, has become an integral part of our daily communication, it is worthwhile to examine how different texting usages are associated with these psychological outcomes.
In sum, the present study aims to contribute to the current literature by suggesting a theoretical model that integrates motivations for using cellphone texting, usage patterns, and psychological consequences. By so doing, the current study illuminates ways in which cellphone texting is embedded in our communication practices, which further account for our relational and psychological well-being.

2. Literature review

2.1. Motivations for cellphone texting and usage patterns

As described earlier, research on the motivations for cellphone texting has been conducted largely within the U&G framework. Many studies have revealed key motivations, including entertainment, affection, fashion, escape, immediate access and mobility, information seeking, and coordination (e.g., Grellhesl and Punyanunt-Carter, 2012, Leung, 2007, Wei, 2008). Some studies have focused more on the motivations of relational communication via cellphone use. These studies have found slightly varied motivations, such as self-presentation and intimacy (Reid & Reid, 2007), personal contact, decreasing loneliness, and social ease (Bardi & Brady, 2010), and affection, inclusion, and control (Jin & Park, 2010). Further, examining the uses of text-based communication media including email, cellphone texting, and Facebook Wall postings, Park, Chung, and Lee (2012) found two different types of motivations across the three media: motivation to communicate with strong tie networks and motivation to communicate with weak tie networks.
In order to identify different motivations, most studies factor-analyzed the statements gleaned from previous studies on traditional telephone or cellphone use (e.g., Grellhesl and Punyanunt-Carter, 2012, Leung, 2007, Reid and Reid, 2007), although some studies (e.g., Bardi and Brady, 2010, Jin and Park, 2010) employed existing scales of motivations for cellphone use such as the Interpersonal Communication Motive scale (Rubin, Perse, & Barbato, 1988).
These different motivations for cellphone texting are expected to affect usage patterns. In terms of examining media usage patterns, traditional media studies focused on the amount of users' time spent on a given medium. Given that some traditional media such as television or radio can be used without users' full attention, a simple measure of time spent on such media has been criticized (McLeod & McDonald, 1985). Yet, cellphone use involves a more purposive behavior compared to mass media use such as television watching. Therefore, it is reasonable to measure an individual's cellphone use with the amount of time dedicated to it. In addition, another measure of texting usage patterns can be useful when considering the characteristics of cellphone use, which primarily involves communication with certain boundaries of people who already know each other: the number of text messages sent and received with these people can indicate how actively cellphone users are engaging in communication with others.
In the present study, we replicate previous studies on the motivations for the use of cellphone texting. Further, we examine how the motivations affect texting usage patterns by considering both users' time spent on cellphone texting and the number of text messages sent and received. The following research questions address these points:
RQ1: What are individuals' motivations for using cellphone texting?
RQ2: How do individuals' motivations affect (a) time spent on cellphone texting and (b) the number of text messages sent and received?

2.2. Cellphone texting and psychological outcomes

Social penetration theory (Altman & Taylor, 1973) posits that increased self-disclosure in interpersonal communication leads to enhanced feelings of intimacy. Perlman and Fehr (1987) defined intimacy as a feeling of closeness resulted from personal disclosures and interactions between communication partners. It is also regarded as a personal and subjective sense of connectedness stemmed from reciprocal interactions (Greene et al., 2006, Laurenceau et al., 2004), and as an essential component in maintaining close relationships (Bauemeister & Leary, 1995).
Cellphone texting takes place typically among people who know each other and therefore can serve as an outlet for self-disclosure. If this is the case, uses of cellphone texting are likely to heighten intimacy. For instance, in the context of instant messaging (IM), Hu, Wood, Smith, and Westbrook (2004) discovered that the amount of IM use was positively associated with affective and social intimacy among friends. It is notable, however, that some studies have found no significant association between the amount of time spent on social networking sites (SNSs) and emotional closeness (e.g., Pollet, Roberts, & Dunbar, 2011). Though not based upon empirical findings, Turkle (2011) also claimed that the use of cellphone has reduced the intimacy of interpersonal relationships. These contrasting findings suggest the following research question:
RQ3: What is the association between the use of cellphone texting (i.e., time spent on cellphone texting and the number of text messages sent and received) and perceived intimacy?
Anderson and Emmers-Sommer (2006) defines relationship satisfaction as “the degree to which an individual is content and satisfied with his or her relationship” (p. 155). With respect to the association between media use and relationship satisfaction, the extant literature offers limited empirical findings. The construct of relationship satisfaction has been utilized mostly in romantic relationships. Further, previous studies have shown inconsistent findings: a few found a negative association between romantic partners' use of new media such as Facebook and relationship satisfaction (e.g., Elphinston and Noller, 2011, Hand et al., 2013), whereas others found no direct association between cellphone use and relationship satisfaction among college students (e.g., Hall & Baym, 2011).
It has been demonstrated that mobile communication via cellphones takes place among intimate and homogeneous peers (Campbell and Kwak, 2012, Ishii, 2006), so-called “tele-cocooning” (Habuchi, 2005), which in turn, makes strong ties stronger and weak ties weaker (Campbell and Kwak, 2012, Ling, 2008). Thus, it is expected that frequent exchanges of text messages enhance relationship satisfaction among the communication partners. A recent study about cellphone texting and social scope directly tested the tele-cocooning hypothesis. Kobayashi and Boase (2014) found a positive association between frequency of texting and general trust in the Japanese context. Acknowledging the inconsistent empirical findings in different contexts, we cautiously hypothesize that:
H1: The use of cellphone texting (i.e., time spent on cellphone texting and the number of text messages sent and received) will be positively associated with relationship satisfaction.
The association between media use and loneliness, which refers to perceived deficiencies in one's ongoing relationships (Peplau et al., 1979, Perlman and Peplau, 1981), has been examined in the context of various media such as television (e.g., Perse and Rubin, 1990, Rubin et al., 1985), the Internet in general (e.g., Kim et al., 2009, Moody, 2001), and SNSs (e.g., Seldon, 2008). Research on the relationship between cellphone use and loneliness has also been accumulated, although many of them investigated the effect of voice calling. For instance, Wei and Lo (2006) found that cellphone use led to a lower level of loneliness. However, a few studies (e.g., Jin and Park, 2013, Reid and Reid, 2007) discovered that cellphone use was associated with increased loneliness. Upon these contrasting findings, Chan (2015) posited that the negative findings of Jin and Park (2013) and Reid and Reid (2007) were possibly due to the measurement of generic cellphone usage without reference to who or why study respondents were calling, whereas the positive finding of Wei and Lo's (2006) study was based upon more specific measurements of socially oriented uses of cellphone. We acknowledge the merits of Chan's (2015) argument and believe that more specific measurements should be pursued in future studies on mobile communication. Given that the findings about cellphone texting can be different from those about voice calling, the aforementioned contrasting results lead us to suggest the following research question:
RQ4: What is the association between the use of cellphone texting (i.e., time spent on cellphone texting and the number of text messages sent and received) and feelings of loneliness?
Previous studies have well-documented the positive association between intimacy and relationship satisfaction in the context of romantic relationships (e.g., Hassebrauck and Fehr, 2002, Huston and Chorost, 1994). Recently, Hand et al. (2013) demonstrated the mediating role of intimacy in the association between SNS use and relationship satisfaction. In the present study, we explore the associations between these two relationship variables (i.e., intimacy and relationship satisfaction) and feelings of loneliness. Studies on these associations are quite scant. Although Reid and Reid (2007) found a negative association between intimacy and loneliness, their study derived the construct of intimacy from an exploratory factor analysis based upon the U&G approach instead of a measurement of intimacy itself. With respect to the association between relationship satisfaction and loneliness, Mellor, Stokes, Firth, Hayashi, and Cummins (2008) found a negative association in an Australian context. Similarly, Flora and Segrin (2000) showed a negative association between the two constructs in dating couples. The present study tests the associations among intimacy, relationship satisfaction, and loneliness in the context of cellphone texting use:
H2: Intimacy will be (a) positively associated with relationship satisfaction and (b) negatively associated with feelings of loneliness.
H3: Relationship satisfaction will be negatively associated with feelings of loneliness.
To sum up, integrating the literature and hypotheses suggested above, the proposed research model of the current study is presented in Fig. 1.

3. Method

3.1. Survey administration

An online survey was conducted in a large public university in the Southwestern United States. From the directory of all students, 1500 were randomly selected. An email invitation was sent to these students, including a link to the survey questionnaire on SurveyMonkey (http://www.surveymonkey.com). After the initial invitation, two follow-up reminders were sent to the students who had not responded. Of the 1500 students, 414 (27.6%) completed the survey and received $5 credit to their on-campus spending account.

3.2. Measures

To identify the motivations for cellphone texting, statements were gathered from past studies that examined the motivations for using landline phone or cellphone (e.g., Park et al., 2012). Subsequently, 11 statements were factor-analyzed for further statistical analyses (see the Results section for the findings from an exploratory factor analysis and Table 1 for the specific statements).

For cellphone use, respondents were asked to indicate the amount of time they spent on cellphone texting in a typical day. They were also asked to estimate the number of text messages they sent and received on an average day. Since the numbers of text messages sent and received per day were highly correlated with each other (r = 0.67, p < 0.01, n = 335), they were summed to make a composite variable.
The average time that respondents spent on cellphone texting was approximately 2 h and 21 min (M = 141.07, SD = 164.41, Median = 90 in minutes), while the average number of text messages sent and received was approximately 110 (M = 109.82, SD = 148.28, Median = 55). Given the non-normality of these two measures of cellphone texting (skewness: 2.55, kurtosis: 7.65 for time spent on cellphone texting; skewness: 2.98, kurtosis: 11.68 for the number of cellphone text messages), log transformations were performed, which resulted in a significant improvement in the normality of the variables (M = 1.90, SD = 0.50 for time spent on cellphone texting; M = 1.71, SD = 0.59 for the number of cellphone text messages). These log-transformed variables were used in the subsequent analyses.
Intimacy was measured with a modified Miller's Social Intimacy Scale (MSIS: Miller & Lefcourt, 1982). Eight questions relevant to the context of cellphone texting included: “When you have leisure time, how often do you choose to spend it with people you communicate with?,” “How much do you feel like being encouraging and supportive to people with whom you communicate?,” and “How often do you feel close to people with whom you communicate?” (Cronbach's α = 0.87). These questions were anchored by a 5-point answer option ranging from “very rarely (or not much) (1)” to “almost always (or a great deal) (5).”
Relationship satisfaction was measured with five questions modified from Hendrick's (1988) Generic Measure of Relationship Satisfaction, which includes: “In general, how well do the people with whom you interact meet your needs?,” “In general, how satisfied are you with your relationship with the people whom you communicate with?,” and “In general, to what extent have your relationships with the people with whom you communicate met your expectations?” (Cronbach's α = 0.74). These questions were anchored by a 5-point answer option ranging from “not at all (1)” to “very much (5).”
Loneliness was measured with the UCLA Loneliness Scale (version 3) (Russell, 1996), which has 20 questions that include: “How often do you feel that you are ‘in tune’ with the people around you?” and “How often do you feel that there is no one you can turn to?” (Cronbach's α = 0.87). These questions were anchored by a 4-point answer option ranging from “never (1)” to “always (4).” Higher scores indicated greater feelings of loneliness after rearrangement of the reversed questionnaire items.

3.3. Data analysis

Structural equation modeling (SEM) was run with the AMOS 18 program to test the hypotheses and build a theoretical model for the associations among the motivations for cellphone texting, usage patterns, and psychological consequences. With maximum likelihood estimation, the following criteria were used to estimate the overall fit of the model (Hu & Bentler, 1999): (1) the chi-square statistic, which is recommended to be nonsignificant; (2) the non-normed fit index (NNFI), which needs to be close to 1.00; (3) the comparative fit index (CFI), which is recommended to be greater than 0.90; and (4) the root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), whose value of less than 0.08 is considered as practical evidence of a good fit (Browne & Cudeck, 1993), although RMSEA of less than 0.05 is a more robust indicator of a good fit.

4. Results

4.1. Descriptive statistics

Among 414 respondents from the survey, 335 were valid ones for subsequent statistical analyses because the rest answered less than half of the survey questions. Of the 335 respondents, there were 208 female respondents (62.1%) and 124 male participants (37.0%), while three did not indicate their sex. The average age of the respondents was 22.75 (SD = 5.95). In terms of race distribution, the number of “White, not of Hispanic origin” was 242 (72.2%), “African American, not of Hispanic origin” was 10 (3.0%), “Hispanic” was 20 (6.0%), “American Indian or Alaska native“ was 16 (4.8%), “Asian or Pacific Islander” was 31 (9.3%), and sixteen respondents did not indicate their race.

4.2. Exploratory factor analysis of motivations

To answer RQ1, an exploratory factor analysis was conducted with 11 motivation statements. Based upon an orthogonal Varimax rotation, two motivation factors for using cellphone texting were extracted: communication with strong-tie network and communication with weak-tie network. Loadings of variables on factors and eigenvalues are presented in Table 1. With the results from the exploratory factor analysis, summed scales were made for subsequent SEM analyses. Cronbach's alphas of the summed scales are also presented in Table 1. To answer RQ2, the association between the two motivations and cellphone use was tested.

4.3. Analysis of structural equation modeling

Prior to SEM analyses, a correlation analysis with all variables was conducted. The correlation matrix used for the proposed model is shown in Table 2. Loneliness was negatively correlated with all other variables in the model. The highest correlation coefficient among the variables was 0.71 (the association between time spent on cellphone texting and the number of text messages), slightly above the recommended threshold of 0.70 (Tabachnik & Fidell, 2001). However, given that these two variables both measure cellphone texting use, the high correlation coefficient is understandable. Overall, it was concluded that variables in the proposed model do not exhibit significant multicollinearity problems.

The results from SEM analyses were as follows. The chi-square statistic was significant, χ2(6, N = 335) = 26.42, p < 0.001. For other indices, NNFI was 0.90, CFI was 0.97, and RMSEA was 0.06. RQ2 addressed the association between motivations and the use of cellphone texting. As presented in Fig. 2, the motivation of communication with strong ties was not significantly associated with time spent on cellphone texting but was significantly associated with the number of text messages sent and received (β = 0.20, p < 0.01). The motivation of communication with weak ties was significantly associated with both time spent on cellphone texting (β = 0.20, p < 0.01) and the number of text messages sent and received (β = 0.16, p < 0.05).
RQ3 dealt with the association between the use of cellphone texting and intimacy. While time spent on cellphone texting was not significantly associated with intimacy, the number of text messages sent and received was positively associated with intimacy (β = 0.21, p < 0.01). H1 discussed the association between the use of cellphone texting and relationship satisfaction. Both time spent on cellphone texting and the number of text messages sent and received were significantly associated with relationship satisfaction. However, it is notable that time spent on cellphone texting was negatively associated with relationship satisfaction (β = −0.25, p < 0.001), whereas the number of text messages sent and received was positively associated with relationship satisfaction (β = 0.15, p < 0.05). Thus, H1 was partially supported.
RQ4 addressed the association between the use of cellphone texting and feelings of loneliness. Neither time spent on cellphone texting nor the number of text messages sent and received was significantly associated with loneliness. H2 discussed the association between intimacy and relationship satisfaction (H2a) as well as between intimacy and feelings of loneliness (H2b). As expected, intimacy was positively associated with relationship satisfaction (β = 0.42, p < 0.01) and negatively associated with loneliness (β = −0.27, p < 0.001), fully supporting H2. H3 predicted the association between relationship satisfaction and feelings of loneliness. The expected negative association was confirmed (β = −0.32, p < 0.001). Therefore, H3 was also supported. 5. Discussion

The present study examined the associations among motivations for using cellphone texting, patterns of cellphone texting use measured by time spent on cellphone texting and the number of text messages sent and received, and psychological outcomes. Further, the study sought to suggest an integrated model that explains their associations. We interpret the findings in greater detail below.

5.1. Summary and implications of the findings

First, motivations for using cellphone texting are similar to those from previous studies (e.g., Park et al., 2012). Considering that mobile communication via cellphone takes place usually within the circle of people who already know each other, the motivations of communication with strong ties and weak ties are not surprising. This finding supports the “tele-cocooning” hypothesis. It is notable, however, that the association between the motivation of communication with weak ties and the amount of time spent on cellphone texting was statistically significant (β = 0.20, p < 0.01), while that between the motivation of communication with strong ties and the amount of time spent on cellphone texting was not. This result indicates that cellphone users do not spend too much time for communication with their close circles, in particular for the goal of emotionally expressive and supportive communication, although they may be exchanging a large number of text messages. Chan (2015) suggested a hint for this finding: the boundary between personal life and work life has been blurred in recent years. Given that the respondents in the current study were college students, they might be utilizing cellphone texting for academic or broader social interaction purposes such as organizing team meetings or campus activities and exchanging information. In these cases, the motivation of communication with weak ties, which primarily involves instrumental rather than expressive purposes, may be associated with a larger amount of time spent for texting because they have to be more carefully paying attention to the messages sent and received. Further, the fact that young people's use of cellphone calling is falling yet their use of cellphone texting is increasing in recent years (Lenhart, 2012) indirectly verifies this speculation. Nevertheless, it is presumable that examining voice calling for communication with strong ties might have produced a different outcome as to its association with time spent on cellphone, a point which future studies are encouraged to investigate.
It is quite surprising that the amount of time spent on cellphone texting was negatively associated with relationship satisfaction (see Fig. 2), meaning that the more an individual spends time on cellphone texting, the higher the extent to which the individual feels discontent in the relationship with his or her communication partners in general. While the results of the current study do not provide a full explanation for this association, it can be speculated that time spent on texting interferes with time that could be potentially spent on other channels of communication that are more desired. Further, as the use of cellphone texting with weak ties has increased in work-based contexts, which does not call for emotional involvement (Chan, 2015), it is understandable that time spent on cellphone texting may not positively contribute to the relationship between communication partners. Another point of consideration is that the measure of time spent on texting adopted in the current study does not distinguish between more self-initiated uses and norm-based uses (e.g., social norms of reciprocating the communication). If the latter case exists, it could explain the negative correlation with relationship satisfaction. Moreover, the amount of time spent on cellphone texting was not significantly associated with either intimacy or loneliness. Overall, the results demonstrate that the amount of time spent on cellphone texting itself does not play an important role in explaining the relational and psychological well-being of individuals. Perhaps, as individuals become to utilize cellphone texting less for emotional or expressive purposes and more for task related and instrumental purposes, positive psychological effects via cellphone texting in relational communication might be weakened. It is necessary for future studies to validate this finding to investigate whether the present study's outcome is unique in this study's context or can be applied in other contexts (e.g., general adult population) as well. One possible agenda will be to further specify the types of people to whom cellphone users are texting and the relational goals that cellphone texting users intend to achieve.
Unlike the amount of time spent on cellphone texting, the number of text messages sent and received played substantial roles. It directly affected intimacy and relationship satisfaction in a positive direction while indirectly influencing loneliness. The results might be explained by the visibility of text messages sent and received, which individuals could use to infer their and their communication partners' level of involvement in their interaction. On the other hand, the measure of time spent on texting does not indicate the amount of time their communication partners spend on their relational communication. Given that intimacy and relationship satisfaction are primarily based on the interactions between communication partners, the number of text messages could be better explaining the relational and psychological outcome variables. The two measures (time spent on texting and the number of messages sent and received) are highly correlated; yet, one is not fully equal to the other. Similar to the suggestion above regarding time spent on texting, a more refined measure of the number of text messages sent and received (e.g., voluntary nature of texting, the proportion of sent versus received messages) would provide a better understanding of the results.
Another notable finding is the association between the use of cellphone texting and loneliness. According to the model developed in the current study, neither time spent on cellphone texting nor the number of text messages was directly associated with loneliness (see Fig. 2). Yet, these two patterns of cellphone texting use were mediated by intimacy and/or relationship satisfaction in their association with loneliness. A closer look at the association between the patterns of cellphone texting use and loneliness (see the correlation coefficients in Table 2), however, suggests that they have significant negative associations, indicating that cellphone texting is related to lower levels of reported feelings of loneliness. Thus, we conducted an additional regression analysis in which the two patterns of cellphone texting use were independent variables while loneliness was an outcome variable. This analysis indicated that time spent on cellphone texting was not a significant factor that affected loneliness, whereas the number of text messages was a significant factor (β = −0.24, p < 0.01), F(2, 332) = 10.38, p < 0.001, R2 = 0.06. This finding is contrary to that from Jin and Park (2013) and Reid and Reid (2007), which found that cellphone use is associated with higher levels of loneliness. We can claim, based upon this finding, that the number of text messages exchanged between communication partners plays a more important role in explaining the psychological well-being of cellphone users, as shown throughout the findings in the present study.
The present study provides theoretical implications. The study aimed to integrate motivations for using cellphone texting, patterns of cellphone text use, and psychological consequences. In doing so, the study found that different types of cellphone text use exhibit different roles in predicting psychological outcomes. Specifically, whereas the amount of time spent on cellphone texting was negatively associated with relationship satisfaction, the number of text messages was positively associated with relationship satisfaction. Although several studies examined the association between cellphone use (voice calling and/or texting) and psychological consequences (e.g., Chan, 2015, Hall and Baym, 2011, Jin and Park, 2010, Jin and Park, 2013, Reid and Reid, 2007, Wei and Lo, 2006), few studies separated different patterns of cellphone use; they usually utilized the motivation variables only, or the frequency or amount of usage time. In contrast, by identifying the different roles of cellphone usage patterns, the present study provides an account of the direction and the extent to which cellphone texting use is channeled into relational and psychological well-being. Furthermore, the current study was able to specify the relative strength of different usage patterns in terms of their influence on psychological outcomes. The number of text messages, rather than time spent on cellphone texting, was a better indicator that explains both intimacy and loneliness. The present study also added a finding, via an additional analysis as well as the integrated model, that the use of cellphone texting was negatively associated with loneliness as opposed to the findings from some previous studies (e.g., Jin and Park, 2013, Reid and Reid, 2007). Particularly, given that Jin and Park's (2013) study examined the role of voice calling with respect to the association between cellphone use and loneliness, the current study, with the association between cellphone texting and loneliness, sheds another light on the study of mobile communication and psychological outcomes.

5.2. Limitations

Several limitations should be mentioned. First, although the present study found that there are two communication motivations for cellphone texting via an exploratory factor analysis, we did not ask with whom respondents exchanged text messages (i.e., strong and weak ties). Should we have known the extent to which respondents feel close to their communication partners, we might have been better able to capture the role played by the usage patterns of cellphone texting in accounting for psychological outcomes. From a similar perspective, conducting an analysis at the dyadic level will allow an examination of how specific relationship types and contexts with one's communication partners moderate the associations between motivations and usage patterns as well as between usage patterns and outcomes.
Second, the current study was conducted with a sample of college students, which represents a unique population from various aspects. College students are engaged in campus life that involves multiple types of instrumental tasks (e.g., academic work, social tasks such as participating in student organizations) in addition to pure relational communication that can be carried via cellphone. If we had diversified the study sample, the results might have been different. Future studies are encouraged to recruit more representative samples to improve generalizability of the findings.
Lastly, given the increasingly diverse platforms for social media communication that are enabled through cellphone, distinguishing between texting and other types of messaging services such as those through SNSs will be useful. The measure of usage patterns could also employ more detailed aspects such as the types of communication content exchanged via texting, the level of self-disclosure, and feelings of reciprocity, which might affect the relational and psychological outcomes examined in the current study.

5.3. Conclusion

In conclusion, the present study examined the associations among motivations for using cellphone texting, usage patterns, and psychological consequences. The study identified motivations similar to those derived from previous studies, and those motivations translated into different patterns of cellphone texting use, which in turn affected intimacy, relational satisfaction, and loneliness. Contrary to expectation, time spent on cellphone texting was negatively associated with relationship satisfaction. In contrast, the number of text messages contributed to reduced feelings of loneliness through a higher level of perceived intimacy and relationship satisfaction. Overall, the current study encourages a more comprehensive view of the predictors and outcomes surrounding uses of cellphone texting. Future research is suggested to validate and elaborate the associations uncovered in the present study.
